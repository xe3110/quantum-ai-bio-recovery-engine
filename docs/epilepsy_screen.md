# Epilepsy combination screen — protocol and results

A deterministic, multi-parameter, discovery-stage screen over a curated panel of
**37 epilepsy candidates** scored at **three orders** — 37 monotherapies, 666
pairs, and 6,174 triples — against an **85-gene directional disease signature**
and a **cached STRING v12 interactome**.

It is **not** a clinical combination recommendation. Antiseizure medication is
taken for decades, often by women of childbearing age, and stopping a drug can
itself provoke seizures. No in-silico score prices teratogenic risk, enzyme
induction, or the cost of a breakthrough seizure. Every output is a hypothesis
for experimental validation.

The standing procedure this run instantiates is in the
[disease campaign protocol](disease_campaign_protocol.md). **This document states
only what is specific to epilepsy.** The companion screens are
[multiple sclerosis](ms_publication_protocol.md),
[Parkinson's](parkinsons_screen.md) and [Alzheimer's](alzheimers_screen.md); the
campaign that designs a molecule rather than ranking existing ones is
[here](denovo_design_protocol.md#epilepsy).

```bash
python -m experiments.epilepsy.run_combination_screen --top 25 --seed 7
python -m experiments.epilepsy.run_combination_screen --quick     # about a minute
pytest -q tests/test_combination_scoring.py tests/test_multi_disease.py
```

## 1. What is different about this disease

Epilepsy inverts the Alzheimer's problem.

| | Alzheimer's | Epilepsy |
|---|---|---|
| Disease-defining targets | tau and APP, the two a small molecule reaches worst | ion channels and receptors, the **most** tractable class |
| Approved agents in the panel | 8 of 36 | **30 of 37** |
| Approved disease-modifying agents | antibodies, recently | **none** (everolimus for TSC-associated seizures only) |
| Heaviest risk | ARIA (brain oedema) | **teratogenicity** |
| Unusual risk | withdrawal-grade toxicity averaged away | **seizure aggravation**: a drug of the right mechanism can worsen a syndrome |

Because the targets are tractable and the panel is approved-heavy, the questions
change. There is no shortage of chemistry against sodium channels, GABA-A
receptors, calcium channels and SV2A. What is missing is anything beyond them:
about a third of patients are drug-resistant, and no approved drug prevents
epileptogenesis.

**The redundancy rule matches clinical practice here.** Two sodium-channel
blockers are excluded as a pair because they share a safety class and a target
family, and rational polytherapy avoids exactly that. Eight agents in the panel
carry the `sodium_channel_blocker` class, so 28 sodium-channel pairs are excluded
by construction. That is a strength for realism and a cost for exploration: a
genuinely useful dual-sodium combination would be invisible to this screen.

## 2. Inputs specific to epilepsy

- **`data/ep_expression.csv`** — 85 genes across 14 pathways.

  > **This signature is weaker evidence than the MS entry's.** It is curated from
  > literature knowledge rather than derived from a cohort, and it **mixes two
  > things a real study would separate**: findings from acquired temporal lobe
  > epilepsy (interneuron loss, KCC2 downregulation, hippocampal sclerosis) and
  > monogenic channelopathies (*SCN1A*, *KCNQ2*, *KCNT1*, *STXBP1*, *DEPDC5*).
  > Epilepsy is a group of disorders, and a single signature averages over
  > mechanisms that call for opposite treatments: sodium-channel blockers help most
  > focal epilepsies and can **worsen** Dravet syndrome, whose cause is loss of
  > *SCN1A* function. `seizure_aggravation` is a risk domain for that reason, but
  > it is a single population-level number per agent.

- **`data/drugs/epilepsy_panel_v1.json`** — 37 records regenerated by
  `python -m tools.curate_epilepsy_panel`, which fails if any target gene is
  absent from the signature. 30 approved, 2 phase 3, 5 phase 2; 32 mechanism
  classes; 41 of 85 signature genes targeted.

  Negative-efficacy controls are **three**, fewer than the other diseases (four
  for MS, five each for Parkinson's and Alzheimer's), because an approved-heavy
  panel has few failures. Each was checked against a source rather than recalled:
  bumetanide (the NEMO neonatal trial missed its primary endpoint and was stopped
  after hearing loss in 3 of 11 surviving infants), soticlestat (phase 3 missed
  its primary endpoints in Dravet and Lennox-Gastaut syndromes), and talampanel (a
  1-year, 300-patient placebo-controlled trial failed to show efficacy; an earlier
  crossover trial had suggested some effect). The safety-penalty controls are
  felbamate (aplastic anaemia and hepatic failure), retigabine (withdrawn
  worldwide in 2017 for retinal and skin pigmentation) and vigabatrin (permanent
  visual-field loss). The one recent positive result in the panel, azetukalner
  (XEN1101), had a positive phase 3 in focal seizures (53.2% seizure reduction
  against 10.4% on placebo at 25 mg).

- **`data/networks/string_ep_network.tsv`** — STRING v12, confidence ≥ 0.4, 234
  nodes, 7,001 edges. **84 of 85 signature genes are present.** `AQP4` is
  missing because **STRING v12 does not recognise that symbol** (a direct lookup
  returns "not found"), not because it lacks interactions. I first recorded this as
  "no interactions at the 0.4 cutoff" without checking, and that was wrong. No alias
  is declared because none is known to resolve it, and the gene contributes to
  signature reversal but not to network proximity.

- **Vocabulary** — eight therapeutic axes (`sodium_channel_stabilisation`,
  `gabaergic_potentiation`, `glutamate_attenuation`, `vesicle_release_modulation`,
  `calcium_channel_modulation`, `potassium_channel_opening`,
  `epileptogenesis_modification`, `neuromodulatory_adjunct`) and nine risk domains
  (`sedation_cognitive`, `dermatologic_hypersensitivity`, `teratogenicity`,
  `hepatic`, `hematologic`, `behavioural_mood`, `cardiac`, `seizure_aggravation`,
  `retinal_toxicity`).

  Teratogenicity carries the highest weight, then severe cutaneous
  hypersensitivity and retinal toxicity, because they are severe and not reliably
  reversible. **No two registered diseases share a therapeutic axis.** The risk
  domains overlap only on generic toxicity: `cardiac`, `hepatic`, and
  `teratogenicity`, which MS shares. That last one is the reason the test's
  generic set was widened; it was a decision, not a rename to pass.

- **Delivery** — `requires_cns_exposure: true`. Efflux at the blood-brain barrier
  is a leading hypothesis for drug-resistant epilepsy, so exposure at the target
  is a live scientific question here, not only a delivery detail.

## 3. Results — monotherapy versus combination

Compared on the efficacy block only. `priority_score` is **not** in this table and
must not be: it includes complementarity and separation terms a single agent cannot
earn by construction (see the [rule](disease_campaign_protocol.md#comparing-across-orders--the-rule)).

| order | n | median reversal | max reversal | % beating best monotherapy | max gain | % sub-additive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 37 | 0.0148 | 0.0308 | — | — | 0% |
| 2 | 615 | 0.0284 | 0.0527 | 38.0% | +0.0219 | 14.5% |
| 3 | 6,174 | 0.0420 | 0.0741 | 89.9% | +0.0433 | 33.4% |

Best reversal at each order: the **P2X7 antagonist exemplar** alone (3.08%,
extrapolated evidence); everolimus + P2X7 antagonist (5.27%); midazolam +
everolimus + P2X7 antagonist (**7.41%**). Restricted to approved agents the best
singles are everolimus (2.19%), midazolam (2.13%), clobazam (2.00%), phenobarbital
(1.99%) and cenobamate (1.90%).

**As a share of what the panel could reach**
([tools/reversal_ceiling.py](../tools/reversal_ceiling.py)): the panel targets 41 of
85 signature genes, a full-reversal ceiling of 43.9% and a pooled-panel ceiling of
30.8%, the lowest full-reversal ceiling of the five diseases (the pooled-panel figure
is second lowest, after Guillain-Barré's 29.8%). The best single, pair and triple are 7.0%,
12.0% and 16.9% of the full ceiling, and 10.0%, 17.1% and 24.1% of the pooled-panel
one. This is a normalisation for comparing diseases, not a clinical calibration;
see [the calibration write-up](efficacy_calibration.md).

### Three readings that matter more than the leaderboard

**1. Third agents almost never earn their place.** Of 6,174 triples, **5,150
(83.4%)** have a *negative* `score_gain_over_best_subset`, with a median gain of
**−0.0706**. The other three diseases were 60.3% (Parkinson's), 33.6% (MS) and
33.0% (Alzheimer's). This is consistent with the clinical advice to use one drug,
then two, and to be slow to add a third, but I do not read it as validation. Several
risk domains are weighted heavily here, and the safety union of a third agent
compounds quickly, so part of the number is the risk arithmetic and not any
benefit judgement.

**2. Combination gain over the best single agent is moderate, and redundancy is
low.** 38.0% of pairs beat their own best member, and only 14.5% of pairs and 33.4%
of triples are sub-additive (Parkinson's: 12.8% and 36.0%; Alzheimer's 28.1% and
62.0%; MS 40.4% and 77.2%), among the lowest redundancy of the four diseases. That
is partly an artefact of the redundancy rule: sodium-channel and GABA-A agents that
would overlap are excluded before they are scored, so what remains is diverse by
construction.

**3. 51 of 666 pairs (7.7%) were excluded as redundant**: 28 among the eight
sodium-channel blockers, 15 among the six GABA-A modulators, 3 among the carbonic
anhydrase inhibitors, and five same-target-class pairs (vigabatrin + tiagabine,
retigabine + azetukalner, gabapentin + pregabalin, levetiracetam + brivaracetam,
perampanel + talampanel). No triple was
excluded, for the usual reason that the order ladder catches redundancy one order
down.

## 4. Mechanism strata

**The unit of inference is the mechanism stratum, not the named combination.**
Bootstrap top-25 Jaccard is **0.358 at order 2 and 0.235 at order 3**, the highest of
the first four diseases (Guillain-Barré's 0.598 at order 2 is a panel-size effect, not
comparable) but still far below the 0.5 the runner treats as stable, so
individual combinations are not a defensible result. Weight sensitivity is the
*lowest* of the five (Spearman 0.951 / 0.942 mean, with minimums of 0.83 and 0.80),
so unlike the other diseases the ranking is somewhat sensitive to the weights too.

Top and bottom strata at order 2:

| axis stratum | n | median | 95% CI | q |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| neuromodulatory_adjunct + potassium_channel_opening | 6 | 1.393 | [1.192, 1.507] | 0.026 |
| calcium_channel_modulation + potassium_channel_opening | 12 | 1.333 | [1.192, 1.386] | 0.0001 |
| epileptogenesis_modification + potassium_channel_opening | 12 | 1.276 | [1.128, 1.449] | 0.0007 |
| potassium_channel_opening + sodium_channel_stabilisation | 24 | 1.270 | [1.128, 1.316] | 6e-05 |
| … | | | | |
| epileptogenesis_modification + epileptogenesis_modification | 15 | 1.035 | [0.848, 1.246] | 1 |
| **glutamate_attenuation + glutamate_attenuation** | 9 | **0.965** | [0.784, 1.140] | 1 |

**Five of the top seven strata contain `potassium_channel_opening`**, which is also
the axis the design campaign's gap analysis rates most unmet (0.92). As in MS and
Parkinson's, the screen and the gap analysis point at the same axis. Two cautions
that matter more than the agreement:

- **The axis has two agents.** Retigabine and azetukalner are the only
  potassium-channel openers in the panel, so the strongest strata rest on an axis
  with n = 2, and stratum sizes are small (6 to 24). Read it as a statement about
  the axis, not about those two drugs.
- **One of the two was withdrawn from every market in 2017.** The top stratum
  therefore depends in part on a drug the scorer does not know is withdrawn (§5).

## 5. Controls, declared before ranking

| Control | Members | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Positive redundancy | Carbamazepine + Oxcarbazepine | **Excluded as expected** |
| Safety penalty | Vigabatrin (**1**), Retigabine (**2**), Felbamate (325) | **Two of three at the very top of the pooled ranking** |
| Negative efficacy | Bumetanide (**21**), Soticlestat (154), Talampanel (429) | **One of three reaches the top 25** |

Ranks are the best position in the pooled primary ranking of orders 2 and 3, as the
runner defines them.

**The safety-penalty control fails outright.** In Alzheimer's, tacrine passed the
runner's own test and failed only when read per order. Here the runner's own test
fails: **vigabatrin is first and retigabine second** in the pooled ranking, and
vigabatrin is #1 of 37 monotherapies, #1 of 615 pairs and #1 of 6,174 triples
(retigabine: 3rd, 3rd and 1st). They are the leading combinations too: the top
order-2 pair is vigabatrin + acetazolamide and the top order-3 triple is vigabatrin
+ retigabine + acetazolamide. Vigabatrin causes permanent visual-field loss;
retigabine was withdrawn for retinal and skin pigmentation.

The reason is visible in the inputs and is a property of the composite, not the
panel:

| agent | targets | reversal efficiency | signature reversal | gene coverage | composite |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vigabatrin | 1 | 0.90 | 0.0075 | 0.008 | 1.313 (1st) |
| Azetukalner | 2 | 0.90 | 0.0191 | 0.021 | 1.309 (2nd) |
| Retigabine | 2 | 0.85 | 0.0181 | 0.021 | 1.272 (3rd) |
| Carbamazepine | 3 | 0.65 | 0.0189 | 0.029 | 1.057 (10th) |

`reversal_efficiency` divides therapeutic movement by the signal an agent actually
engages, so an agent with **one well-aligned target** scores close to 1.0 however
little of the signature it moves. Vigabatrin's signature reversal is 0.0075, less
than half of carbamazepine's, and it wins on the composite anyway. The panel has
many narrow agents (vigabatrin, tiagabine, acetazolamide, retigabine, azetukalner),
so the composite's preference for a clean, narrow, aligned effect fills the
leaderboard with them. Their safety burden does not stop it: vigabatrin's safety
union (0.229) is below carbamazepine's (0.389), because a single severe domain is
averaged across nine.

I have **not** tuned the scorer to make these controls pass, because that would be
fitting the method to its own check. The finding is that this composite can put a
niche agent with a severe, specific toxicity at the top of a ranking, and that
`priority_score` is not an efficacy ranking: the top composite pair reverses 1.17%
of the signature and the best reversal pair reverses 5.27%.

**Bumetanide reaching the top 25 is the known limitation, reported not filtered.**
Its neonatal trial missed its endpoint and was stopped for hearing loss. It ranks
21st because it has one clean target (`SLC12A2`) and the same narrow-and-aligned
advantage. Its very low modelled CNS penetration (0.1) is a plausible contributor to
the clinical failure, and I have not checked whether the composite prices it
adequately. Soticlestat and talampanel, the other two negatives, are
well outside the top 25. **Mechanistic coherence is not evidence of clinical
benefit.**

## 6. Where this screen ends and design begins

The design campaign runs over the same epilepsy disease model. Its axis-gap
analysis, derived from the panel's *approved* agents rather than from any
combination score, reports:

| therapeutic axis | unmet fraction |
|---|---|
| sodium_channel_stabilisation | 0.00 |
| gabaergic_potentiation | 0.17 |
| calcium_channel_modulation | 0.50 |
| glutamate_attenuation | 0.75 |
| neuromodulatory_adjunct | 0.75 |
| vesicle_release_modulation | 0.83 |
| epileptogenesis_modification | 0.83 |
| potassium_channel_opening | 0.92 |

**Epilepsy is the first disease with no axis at exactly 1.00.** Everolimus
(approved for TSC-associated seizures) and retigabine (approved, then withdrawn)
give even the least-served axes some approved cover. The second is a limitation,
not a fact: a drug withdrawn from every market still counts as cover in this
analysis, which is the same withdrawal blind spot seen in Alzheimer's with
tacrine.

The screen and the gap analysis agree on the potassium-channel axis (§4). The
design results are in [the design protocol](denovo_design_protocol.md#epilepsy).

## 8. Known limitations specific to epilepsy

The [standing limitations](disease_campaign_protocol.md#7-standing-limitations-of-every-campaign)
apply. What is different here:

- **The signature averages over syndromes** (§2). It cannot represent the fact that
  the right drug for one syndrome is the wrong drug for another.
- **No pharmacokinetic or drug-drug-interaction model, and this is where it
  bites hardest of any disease so far.** Carbamazepine, phenytoin and phenobarbital
  induce hepatic enzymes and lower the levels of most co-prescribed drugs, and
  valproate raises lamotrigine levels enough to change its rash risk. The
  regimen-level constraints that govern real polytherapy are pharmacokinetic, and
  `regimen_burden` represents only route and half-life spread.
- **Withdrawal is invisible to the score** (retigabine, and in Alzheimer's,
  tacrine), and here it also distorts the axis-gap analysis.
- **Some panel mechanisms are unsettled.** Whether levetiracetam potentiates or
  inhibits SV2A function, cenobamate's GABA-A action, and cannabidiol's targets are
  disputed, and the corresponding `target_effects` signs are hypotheses with
  `target_uncertainty` set to match.
- **Symptomatic agents dominate the panel** (30 of 37), so any ranking is mostly a
  ranking of antiseizure drugs against each other, and says little about
  disease modification.
- **Redundancy exclusion removes sodium-channel pairs by construction** (§1).
- **The composite rewards narrow, well-aligned agents** (§5). It put vigabatrin and
  retigabine, both with severe specific toxicities, at the top of every order.
  Read the efficacy block, not `priority_score`, for anything resembling efficacy.
- **The strongest stratum rests on an axis with two agents, one of them
  withdrawn** (§4).

## 9. Required validation before publication

1. Stratify by syndrome (focal, generalised, Dravet, Lennox-Gastaut, TSC) before
   any signature is built; a pooled signature answers no clinical question.
2. Replace the signature with discovery and validation cohorts, by syndrome.
3. Re-derive `target_effects` from measured pharmacology.
4. **Build the PK/DDI model** before any combination is proposed for in-vivo work.
5. Add a max-domain safety term and a `withdrawn` flag so withdrawal-grade toxicity
   cannot be averaged away or counted as cover, and cap or reweight
   `reversal_efficiency` so that an agent with one target cannot win on it. Evaluate
   both on all four diseases together, not tuned to this one.
6. Test in disease-relevant systems (patient-derived neurons, organotypic
   hippocampal slices), and validate in a seizure model matched to the syndrome
   (kindling, pilocarpine, or a genetic model such as *Scn1a*+/-).
7. Release code, frozen inputs, seed, full rankings including excluded
   combinations, and the control analysis.

## Where quantum computing is and is not used

**Not used: the combination screen.** The k = 1, 2, 3 screen (37 monotherapies, 666 pairs, 6,174 triples) is classical.

*Why.* (1) The screen enumerates every combination exhaustively, so the answer is exact
and there is no search problem for a quantum heuristic to help with; the full run takes about fourteen minutes.
(2) The expensive part is statistical scoring (the permutation null, bootstrap stability,
weight sensitivity), which is classical and has no quantum counterpart here. (3) The
drug-selection problem can be written as a QUBO (the project does this, see below), but at
this size exact enumeration is faster and gives the true optimum, so there is nothing for
a quantum solver to improve on. Quantum optimisation could matter for much larger
selections (many agents, high order); none of the panels here is in that regime.

**Used, as a benchmark only: the design campaign's fragment selection.** The 10-variable arm-selection QUBO gives +0.0868 on all three solvers; the depth sweep is d1 +0.0868, **d2 −0.0573**, then +0.0868 at depths 3 to 5, so QAOA dipped at depth 2 and recovered.
Enumeration finishes in about 0.07 s; the QAOA depth sweep takes 5 to 6 s. The arm sets
carried into molecule assembly come from **enumeration**, not from QAOA, and QAOA's output
is not used downstream.

*Why only a benchmark.* At 10 binary variables there are 1,024 states, which exhaustive
search covers instantly, so no quantum advantage can be demonstrated or is expected. The
benchmark checks that the Hamiltonian formulation is faithful and transfers to a quantum
algorithm, and that is all it shows.

**Used for higher-order regimen selection (k = 4 to 6), as a benchmark against ground truth.**
The k = 1, 2, 3 screen above stops at k = 3 because it scores every combination. The project's
Hamiltonian machinery has since been pointed at the question beyond it, which k agents from a
pool, written as a QUBO and solved by enumeration, the exact eigensolver, QAOA (penalty and
constraint-preserving variants) and classical baselines. Every answer is re-scored with the true
scorer and ranked against all feasible subsets. It runs on a classical simulator, so no advantage
can be shown. For epilepsy the pool is the 18 best of 37 eligible agents. The surrogate degrades with k (Spearman 0.93, 0.85, 0.75) and its optimum ranks 11th, 61st and 513th. The constrained QAOA matched exact enumeration at k = 4 and 5 but not at k = 6 (713 against 513), and CVaR matched it at all three. Greedy found a better regimen than the surrogate's own optimum at k = 5 (rank 10 against 61), which is the surrogate's error showing. The best pool-limited six-drug regimen contains retigabine and vigabatrin, the two failed safety controls above, so it is the known composite failure again and not a recommendation. Method, caveats and the full tables are in
[the regimen-selection document](quantum_regimen_selection.md).

**Never used: real quantum hardware, and molecule assembly.** QAOA ran on a classical
simulator (Qiskit Aer), and the assembly step that builds the new structures is a classical
stochastic search. None of the findings in this document depends on the quantum solvers: the regimens it reports come from exhaustive classical scoring, and the quantum solvers are judged on whether they recover them.
